A learned gradient gate stops URVFL from reconstructing the passive client's data

The passive client runs a small reinforcement-learning policy between the gradient it receives and its own model update. Against the original URVFL attack, the attacker's reconstruction error rises from 0.031 to 0.390, while honest VFL training is accepted untouched.

Reconstruction error during the URVFL attack

Test-set reconstruction MSE after each attack epoch, measured with the repository's own evaluation. Without a defence the attacker's error falls to 0.031. Simple clipping makes the attack slightly better, ending at 0.024, because capped updates act like a gentler learning rate. The RL policy rejects the attacker's gradients from the first round, so the passive model never moves toward the attacker's shadow space. SplitGuard raised an alarm at round 140 and stopped training, so the attacker kept the reconstruction it had at that point (MSE 0.184, against 0.390 with the RL policy). The attacker's 140 accepted rounds before the alarm were enough to leak coarse structure.

Attacks the policy never trained on

The policy was trained only on simulated URVFL with uniform or class-skewed auxiliary data, plus honest training. The rows marked held out change the attacker's auxiliary data, the discriminator and training schedule (URVFL-sync), or the attack itself (FSHA, AGN).

FSHA was never part of training, yet the policy rejected every FSHA round and the attacker's error stayed at 0.258 against 0.066 undefended. The one failure is URVFL-sync. The policy rejected it for epochs 1 to 4, which held the attacker's error near 0.13, then switched to accepting from epoch 5 and the attack finished at 0.017 against 0.013 undefended. Unlike the URVFL the policy trained on, the sync attacker keeps training its own shadow model and decoder while the passive model is frozen, so its gradients drift into a pattern the policy never saw. AGN did not produce usable reconstructions in this setup even without a defence (MSE 0.66 undefended), so its row says little about any defence. The policy still rejected every AGN round.

Honest VFL keeps working

Honest split training with the repository's top model, run for 10 epochs. The RL policy accepted every honest round, so the defended run receives exactly the honest updates. The small differences between the curves come from GPU nondeterminism, not from the defence. On honest training, SplitGuard raised no false alarm, but its label-flipped fake batches cost accuracy: 45.6% after 10 epochs against 52.1% without it.

How the defence works

active partyReturns gradient ghonest task loss, or the URVFL discriminator loss
RL gate (ours)Builds a state, picks an actionbackward hook on h plus a wrapped optimizer
passive clientApplies the gated updateor skips the step
AcceptApply the gradient normally.
DownweightScale the gradient and the resulting parameter update by 0.3.
ClipClip per-sample gradients and cap the update at half its running norm.
RejectDrop the gradient and skip the optimizer step.

What the policy observes

  • Gradient statistics. These include norm, spread, kurtosis, frequency content and pairwise similarity.
  • Gradient history. Honest task gradients change direction from batch to batch. URVFL pushes the embedding the same way every round, with a cosine to the running mean near 0.87 versus 0.03 for honest training.
  • Reference-encoder drift. A separate encoder trained only on the passive client's own images gives a local baseline. Under URVFL the main embedding becomes markedly more similar to it, with a CKA near 0.83 versus 0.62.

How it was trained

  • PPO in a simulator that calls the repository's own attack step functions, alternating honest and malicious active parties.
  • The reward follows the research concept. The reconstruction term uses the simulated attacker's decoder offline and is never visible at deployment.
  • Each round's reward measures what the chosen action itself changed, by evaluating a probe batch with and without that round's update.
R = −α·Ltask + β·Ereconstruction − γ·Lfalse-rejection − δ·Coverhead

Scope and caveats

  • The original URVFL attack code is unmodified. The defence attaches from outside by wrapping the passive model and its optimizer.
  • SplitGuard as shipped in the repository averages a zero for every regular batch into its detection window, so it fires after about 15 batches on any run. The comparison here uses a corrected copy with the same protocol and threshold.
  • FSHA and AGN are not in the training mix. Over the first rounds, accepting their gradients raises reconstruction error against the attacker's still-untrained decoder, so a per-round reward would teach the policy to accept them. They serve as unseen attacks instead.
  • The policy does not yet hold up against URVFL-sync for a full run. Adding a sync-style attacker, whose shadow model and decoder keep training, to the simulator is the direct fix.
  • All numbers come from a single seed on one GPU. Repeating with more seeds, and testing an adaptive attacker that knows the gate, are the natural next steps.
